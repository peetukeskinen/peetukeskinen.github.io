      What the rules ask for

      EU countries agree a fiscal plan with the Commission that sets a limit on how quickly public spending can grow. The limit has to be tight enough to put public debt on a declining path.

      The required adjustment is worked out by projecting the debt ratio under several different scenarios. The budget position has to improve enough each year for debt to start falling and to keep falling afterwards. That annual adjustment is the number shown at the top of the page.

      Several requirements apply at the same time, and the strictest one determines the overall adjustment. The bars below the chart show what each requirement would imply on its own. Two of them are annual safeguards, so they can make the required adjustment in an individual year larger than in the others.

      The rules

      • Debt must fall in each of the ten years after the plan under four scenarios: the plan as written, weaker fiscal discipline afterwards, higher interest rates combined with lower growth, and a period of market stress.
      • Five years after the plan, debt must also be falling in most of 1,000 simulations covering a range of favourable and adverse shocks.
      • Debt must decline during the plan itself. The required reduction is 0.5 percentage points of GDP per year when debt is between 60% and 90% of GDP, and 1 percentage point when debt is above 90%. For Finland, this is usually the binding requirement.
      • If the previous year's deficit was above 3% of GDP, the adjustment in the current year must be at least 0.5 percentage points.
      • Until the structural deficit is below 1.5% of GDP, the annual adjustment must be at least 0.4 percentage points, or 0.25 percentage points under a seven-year plan.

      Where the numbers come from

      The calculations use the Commission's 2024 guidance for Finland (European Commission prior guidance, 2024), with the plan beginning in 2025. The methodology follows the Commission's Debt Sustainability Monitor.

      Euro amounts are based on Finland's 2023 GDP of €273 billion (Eurostat) and then grow with the projection.

      This is a browser-based version of my MATLAB tool. With the default settings, it reproduces the results published in the National Audit Office's Fiscal Policy Monitoring Report 2024: an adjustment of 0.76 percentage points per year over seven years, about 0.3 percentage points without the debt safeguard, and a spending-growth ceiling of roughly 1.5%.

      Worth knowing

      • This is a personal project, not an official calculation, and it does not represent any institution.
      • Changing a control does not load a different forecast. It shows what the rules would require if that assumption were changed while the rest of the setup remained the same.
      • The fiscal multiplier and inflation path are assumptions, not measured quantities. The 2024 monitoring report argued that both deserve further analysis, which is why they can be varied here.
      • Interest rates move the projection slowly because the Commission's data assume only 3.2–3.8% of long-term debt matures each year. Running the Commission's own formula on Eurostat figures for Finland gives about 6%, so a rate shock probably costs more than shown here.
      • The simulations use the same fixed set of 1,000 draws each time. This means everyone sees the same fan chart, and reloading the page does not change the result.
      • The browser model follows the published MATLAB code, including its simplifications. In particular, it applies the deficit benchmark in structural primary terms throughout and determines the safeguard band from the 2023 debt ratio.

EU fiscal rules calculator

The reformed EU fiscal rules turn on a single operational commitment: each member state agrees a medium-term fiscal-structural plan that fixes how fast net expenditure may grow, and that path has to leave debt on a plausibly declining trajectory. How much consolidation this takes is the output of a debt sustainability analysis rather than a fixed deficit target, so it moves with interest rates, growth and the starting debt ratio — and a set of safeguards can bind before the analysis itself does.

This page runs that analysis for Finland in your browser, following the European Commission's Debt Sustainability Monitor methodology. Move a control and watch the debt trajectory bend, the required adjustment change, and the binding rule switch. Finland's own plan, endorsed by the Council in January 2025, spreads the adjustment over seven years — the setting the page opens with.

An independent replication for teaching and illustration, built from my open MATLAB tool. It is personal work: not an official calculation, and endorsed by no institution. The figures that bind Finland are the Commission's, and under the reformed framework Finland's independent fiscal institution — the body that assesses whether the plan is being kept to — is the Economic Policy Council.

      Net expenditure growth — what the plan commits to

      A country's plan does not commit to the adjustment above directly. It commits to a ceiling on how fast net primary expenditure may grow — spending excluding interest, cyclical unemployment costs and EU-funded items, counted after any new tax measures. The ceiling is the same requirement in another currency: nominal potential growth, less the adjustment scaled by how large primary expenditure is. It is what the Commission monitors a plan against, which is why it is here; for reading the economics, the adjustment above is the clearer number.

      How it works

      The framework works in two steps. The Commission's debt sustainability analysis produces a consolidation need for the adjustment period: the improvement in the structural primary balance, in points of GDP a year, that puts debt on a plausibly declining path. That need is then converted into a reference trajectory for net expenditure — potential growth plus inflation, less the consolidation need scaled by the size of primary expenditure — and the net expenditure path a country commits to may not exceed it. The headline on this page is the first quantity; the second is under “Net expenditure growth” above.

      The consolidation need is raised until every criterion in force is met at once. Meeting the strictest one automatically satisfies the rest, so exactly one criterion binds — which is what the bars under the chart show. Two of them are reviewed each year rather than once for the whole plan: the deficit resilience safeguard and the corrective arm's deficit benchmark. An annual review can only tighten a year, never loosen it, which is why single years in the table can sit above the constant adjustment.

      The criteria are:

      • Deterministic DSA. Debt has to fall in every year of the ten-year review period in four scenarios: the baseline, a permanent slippage of the structural primary balance, permanently worse interest rates and growth, and a temporary episode of market stress. The baseline also has to bring the headline deficit below 3% of GDP during the plan and keep it there.
      • Stochastic DSA. Shocks to growth, the primary balance and interest rates are simulated for the five years after the plan — the shaded fan. Debt has to be lower after those five years than at the end of the plan in the chosen share of simulated paths.
      • Debt sustainability safeguard. Debt has to fall on average by at least 0.5 pp of GDP a year while it is between 60% and 90% of GDP, and 1 pp while it is above 90%. This is stricter than the DSA criteria, which ask debt to fall only after the plan: for Finland it means a fall of 3.5 pp over an extended seven-year plan, or 2 pp over four years. On the chart it is the dashed slope across the plan window that the path has to beat.
      • Deficit benchmark (corrective arm). At least 0.5 pp of adjustment in a year if the deficit exceeded 3% of GDP the year before.
      • Deficit resilience safeguard. At least 0.4 pp a year (0.25 pp under an extended plan) until the structural balance reaches −1.5% of GDP.

      The calculator searches, in steps of 0.01 up to 2.00 pp of GDP a year, for the smallest constant adjustment that passes the DSA criteria and the debt safeguard, then lets the two annually reviewed rules lift individual years on top. Debt is projected with the Commission's own accounting: the implicit interest rate is rebuilt each year from the maturity structure of the debt, adjustment costs output through a fiscal multiplier, the output gap closes gradually, and ageing costs are carried through the review period. Everything here is a projection rather than a forecast — a path under stated assumptions, not the most likely outcome — and the assumptions that move it most are the ones on the controls, together with projections for potential growth and the stock-flow adjustment, the debt changes that do not pass through the deficit and which weigh unusually heavily in Finland's case.

      The grey line on the chart is the reference trajectory — the Commission's baseline assumptions, or a scenario you have pinned — so that any change is seen against something that stays still.

      Against the published calculation. At the assumptions shipped here, this page reproduces the figures in the National Audit Office of Finland's fiscal policy monitoring report 2024, chapter 3: a consolidation need of 0.76 pp of GDP a year with the debt sustainability safeguard binding, about 0.3 pp under the DSA criteria alone, net expenditure growth of about 1.5% a year under a seven-year plan and about zero under a four-year one, and the 2025 exception in which the corrective arm lifts the first year to 0.5 pp because the deficit exceeded 3% in 2024. Switching the debt safeguard off, or the stock-flow adjustment back to the old zero assumption, reproduces the alternatives that report puts beside them.

      Data and limitations

      • Data: European Commission prior guidance, 2024, for Finland, with the plan starting in 2025. Changing the controls moves the assumptions away from that vintage; it does not load a newer forecast.
      • The simulated shocks are a fixed set of 1,000 draws, generated once. The normal option draws them from the historical covariance of quarterly growth, balance and interest rate innovations, with outliers beyond three standard deviations clipped first as in the MATLAB tool; the bootstrap option resamples two-year blocks of the annual historical deviations. Both sets are fixed, so everyone loading the page sees the same result. They are not drawn afresh in the browser, and they are not the MATLAB tool's own draws.
      • Euro amounts need one input the Commission workbook does not carry: the level of nominal GDP. The base year is anchored to Eurostat's current-price GDP for Finland in 2023 (€273 bn, series nama_10_gdp), and every later year is that level carried forward by the model's own nominal growth, so the euro figures move with the growth and inflation assumptions. That anchor is a later data vintage than the 2024 workbook, which shifts the euro amounts by a few tenths of a percent.
      • The model reproduces the published MATLAB tool, including its simplifications. It applies the deficit benchmark in structural primary terms throughout, rather than switching to structural balance terms partway through, and it takes the safeguard's 0.5 or 1 pp band from the 2023 debt ratio rather than from debt at the start of the plan.
      • Results are illustrative. The Commission's own calculation uses the full country data, its own forecast vintage and judgement that no replication reproduces exactly — but at the shipped assumptions the headline figures here match those published in the National Audit Office's monitoring report, as set out above.
      • The fiscal multiplier and the inflation path are assumptions, not estimates. The National Audit Office's 2024 monitoring report argued that the modelling of the multiplier should be reviewed and that the inflation path should rest more on country-specific factors; the controls let you see how much either would change the requirement.

      The method follows the Commission's Debt Sustainability Monitor 2023, and the code benefited from the Python implementation by Darvas et al. (2023). The MATLAB version this page replicates is DSA 5.1.
